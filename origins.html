Origins of Tarot

Historical records, literary texts, and archaeological evidence from the 9th century CE to the 16th century CE document the evolution of playing cards from a simple parlor game into a complex system of symbolic imagery. This section explores the global pre-history of playing cards, the Renaissance allegorical framework of Petrarch’s I Trionfi, and the timeless cross-cultural archetypes that modern readers recognize as The Fool’s Journey.

Pre-History of Playing Cards

Tang Dynasty China (9th Century CE): The "Leaf Game"

Long before Renaissance nobility commissioned painted Italian decks, the physical medium of playing cards was invented in China.

  • The Innovation:

    The earliest credible references to card play appear in late Tang Dynasty texts. In 868 CE, documents describe Princess Tongchang playing a "leaf game" (yezi xi) with members of the royal clan.

  • The Materiality:

    Modeled after early paper currency, these paper "leaves" were manufactured using early woodblock printing. They evolved into "money cards" featuring suits representing single coins, strings of cash, and multiples of currency.

The Transcontinental Journey to Europe

The deck of cards did not materialize out of thin air in Italy. It migrated across continents over several centuries:

  • The Silk Road:

    Chinese card game variants traveled westward along major trade networks through India and Persia.

  • The Mamluk Sultanate (13th Century):

    Islamic merchants in Egypt refined the system into highly structured 52-card decks. Due to religious prohibitions on depicting human forms, these decks used complex geometric and calligraphic designs. They featured four suits: Polo Sticks, Coins, Swords, and Cups.

  • The European Arrival (1370s):

    Trade and military interaction brought these Egyptian cards to European ports like Venice and Barcelona. Within decades, local card makers adapted these shapes into the standard Italian suits that laid the groundwork for Tarocchi.

Petrarch’s Triumphs and the Major Arcana

FFrancesco Petrarch’s 14th-century allegorical poem, I Trionfi ("The Triumphs"), and 15th-century northern Italian court records documenting early Carte da Trionfi card games.

The Renaissance Allegory

The 22 cards we know today as the Major Arcana were originally called carte da trionfi (cards of the triumphs). Their structure directly mirrors the psychological and spiritual hierarchy found in Francesco Petrarch's celebrated 14th-century poem, I Trionfi. In both the poem and the card game, each stage represents a force that conquers the previous one, charting a journey from earthly desire to divine eternity.

The Hierarchical Alignment

  • The Triumph of Love (Amor): Represents youth, earthly desire, and instinct. Tarocchi Parallel: The Lovers card.
  • The Triumph of Chastity (Pudor): Virtue, restraint, and mental control triumph over base desire. Tarocchi Parallel: The Chariot or Temperance cards.
  • The Triumph of Death (Mors): The great equalizer that conquers all mortal achievements and social status. Tarocchi Parallel: The Death card (historically depicting popes and emperors trapped beneath the horse's hooves).
  • The Triumph of Fame (Fama): Memory, legacy, and good deeds outlast physical mortality. Tarocchi Parallel: The Judgment or The Angel card, calling forth historical memory.
  • The Triumph of Time (Tempus): Time eventually erodes all earthly fame, rendering human monuments to dust. Tarocchi Parallel: The Hermit card (originally called "Il Tempo" / Time, frequently carrying an hourglass).
  • The Triumph of Eternity (Eternitas): The ultimate awakening, rising above the material world into permanent divine order. Tarocchi Parallel: The World card.

Literary Parallels: Medieval Archetypes and Humanity's Journey

Geoffrey Chaucer’s 14th-century middle English literary masterpiece, The Canterbury Tales, paired with 20th-century mythic storytelling frameworks regarding universal human paths.

The Shared Framework Narrative

Before modern psychology defined archetypes, medieval and Renaissance literature used them to explore the human condition. A prime literary example is Geoffrey Chaucer’s 14th-century masterpiece, The Canterbury Tales. Chaucer utilizes a classic framework narrative: a diverse group of pilgrims travel together on a spiritual journey, sharing stories that reveal their inner virtues, flaws, and motivations.

While Chaucer wrote completely independently of the Tarot (which was invented decades later in Italy), his literary pilgrimage beautifully mirrors what modern readers call "The Fool's Journey." In modern Tarot philosophy, the Major Arcana acts as a visual pilgrimage. The Fool (Card 0) is the ultimate, naive traveler who walks through the world, encountering distinct archetypal figures to gain wisdom and achieve spiritual completion.

Literary and Tarot Archetype Parallels

When we compare Chaucer's pilgrims to the cards, we see the universal themes that both medieval writers and Renaissance card makers were drawing from:

  • The Knight and The Chariot:

    The Knight represents the peak of medieval chivalry, discipline, honor, and order. He is driven by duty and a strict moral code. This mirrors The Chariot (and The Emperor) archetype, signifying willpower, focused ambition, victory, and control over opposing forces.

  • The Prioress and The High Priestess:

    The Prioress is an educated, refined nun. While she is quiet and bound to her religious structure, she carries an air of courtly romance and hidden depth. This echoes The High Priestess archetype, representing intuition, divine mystery, and a quiet retreat from the chaotic external world.

  • The Wife of Bath and The Empress:

    Wealthy, fiercely independent, and five-times-married, the Wife of Bath is unapologetic about her earthly desires, sexual power, and love of life. She perfectly embodies The Empress archetype, which represents ultimate abundance, feminine power, maternal creation, and sensory pleasures.

  • The Parson and The Hierophant:

    Unlike the corrupt clergy members on the trip, the Parson is a genuinely holy country priest who leads his flock by moral example. He aligns with The Hierophant archetype, representing traditional spiritual wisdom, formal instruction, and institutionalized morality.

  • The Pardoner and The Devil:

    The Pardoner is a manipulative church official who sells fake religious relics and official "pardons" for sins, driven entirely by greed. He represents The Devil archetype (or a reversed Magician), signifying spiritual deception, being chained to material greed, and the toxic illusions that trap the human soul.

Historical Note

The Custom of Tarocchi Appropriati (1500s)

Centuries before modern psychology or occultism viewed the deck as a system for charting human personality, Renaissance elites were already using the cards for interpersonal character mapping.

In the mid-16th century, a popular parlor game called tarocchi appropriati emerged in Italian courts. In this game, courtiers would randomly draw a trump card from the pile, and local court poets would immediately compose extemporaneous, witty verses matching the structural meaning of that specific card to the person’s actual virtues, flaws, or social reputation.

For example, if a courtier drew the Fool, the poet might compose a humorous verse about that person’s naivety or impulsive behavior. If they drew the Emperor, the verse might praise their leadership skills or criticize their authoritarian tendencies. This practice demonstrates that Renaissance elites were already using the cards as a tool for social commentary and personality mapping, long before Tarot became associated with formal divination.
