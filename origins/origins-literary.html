Literary Parallels: Medieval Archetypes and Humanity's Journey

GEOFFREY CHAUCER’S 14TH-CENTURY MIDDLE ENGLISH LITERARY MASTERPIECE, THE CANTERBURY TALES, PAIRED WITH 20TH-CENTURY MYTHIC STORYTELLING FRAMEWORKS REGARDING UNIVERSAL HUMAN PATHS.

The Shared Framework Narrative

Before modern psychology defined archetypes, medieval and Renaissance literature used them to explore the human condition. A prime literary example is Geoffrey Chaucer’s 14th-century masterpiece, The Canterbury Tales. Chaucer utilizes a classic framework narrative: a diverse group of pilgrims travel together on a spiritual journey, sharing stories that reveal their inner virtues, flaws, and motivations.

While Chaucer wrote completely independently of the Tarot (which was invented decades later in Italy), his literary pilgrimage beautifully mirrors what modern readers call "The Fool's Journey." In modern Tarot philosophy, the Major Arcana acts as a visual pilgrimage. The Fool (Card 0) is the ultimate, naive traveler who walks through the world, encountering distinct archetypal figures to gain wisdom and achieve spiritual completion.

Literary and Tarot Archetype Parallels

When we compare Chaucer's pilgrims to the cards, we see the universal themes that both medieval writers and Renaissance card makers were drawing from:

  • The Knight and The Chariot: The Knight represents the peak of medieval chivalry, discipline, honor, and order. He is driven by duty and a strict moral code. This mirrors The Chariot (and The Emperor) archetype, signifying willpower, focused ambition, victory, and control over opposing forces.
  • The Prioress and The High Priestess: The Prioress is an educated, refined nun. While she is quiet and bound to her religious structure, she carries an air of courtly romance and hidden depth. This echoes The High Priestess archetype, representing intuition, divine mystery, and a quiet retreat from the chaotic external world.
  • The Wife of Bath and The Empress: Wealthy, fiercely independent, and five-times-married, the Wife of Bath is unapologetic about her earthly desires, sexual power, and love of life. She perfectly embodies The Empress archetype, which represents ultimate abundance, feminine power, maternal creation, and sensory pleasures.
  • The Parson and The Hierophant: Unlike the corrupt clergy members on the trip, the Parson is a genuinely holy country priest who leads his flock by moral example. He aligns with The Hierophant archetype, representing traditional spiritual wisdom, formal instruction, and institutionalized morality.
  • The Pardoner and The Devil: The Pardoner is a manipulative church official who sells fake religious relics and official "pardons" for sins, driven entirely by greed. He represents The Devil archetype (or a reversed Magician), signifying spiritual deception, being chained to material greed, and the toxic illusions that trap the human soul.

Historical Note: The Custom of Tarocchi Appropriati (1500s)

Centuries before modern psychology or occultism viewed the deck as a system for charting human personality, Renaissance elites were already using the cards for interpersonal character mapping. In the mid-16th century, a popular parlor game called tarocchi appropriati emerged in Italian courts. In this game, courtiers would randomly draw a trump card from the pile, and local court poets would immediately compose extemporaneous, witty verses matching the structural meaning of that specific card to the person’s actual virtues, flaws, or social reputation.

For example, if a courtier drew the Fool, the poet might compose a humorous verse about that person’s naivety or impulsive behavior. If they drew the Emperor, the verse might praise their leadership skills or criticize their authoritarian tendencies. This practice demonstrates that Renaissance elites were already using the cards as a tool for social commentary and personality mapping, long before Tarot became associated with formal divination.
