Evolution of Tarot

The Enlightenment Transformation

The spiritual pilgrimage did not jump straight from the Middle Ages to the modern era. Its most critical evolution occurred in 18th-century France. During the Enlightenment, Tarot transformed from a literal medieval social ladder into a deeply symbolic map of the human subconscious. This shift was born in the aristocratic salons of Versailles and Paris, where rational science collided with a fascination for ancient mystery.

The Renaissance Salons: The Seeds of Esotericism

To understand how Tarot became mystical, one must look to the French intellectual culture of the mid-1700s.

The Culture: The famous Parisian salon culture hosted the intellectual elite alongside charismatic, mysterious figures like the alchemist Count of St. Germain.

The Catalyst: This unique environment blended Freemasonry, early archaeology, and high-society occultism. While elite figures of the era like Madame de Pompadour championed the arts and philosophy, the salons themselves created the perfect melting pot for old traditions to be completely re-imagined through a mystical lens.

Antoine Court de Gébelin (1781): The Egyptian Myth is Born

In 1781, a French scholar, former Protestant minister, and Freemason named Antoine Court de Gébelin forever changed the course of Tarot history.

The Discovery: While visiting a salon in Paris, de Gébelin observed a group of aristocrats playing the old game of Tarot. Looking at the ancient imagery, he concluded the cards were a surviving fragment of a lost ancient civilization.

The Theory: In the eighth volume of his massive encyclopedia, Le Monde Primitif, de Gébelin published the first documented essay claiming Tarot was the sacred, lost "Book of Thoth" from Ancient Egypt. He argued the Major Arcana were hieroglyphs containing timeless spiritual secrets.

The Impact: Though his theory lacked historical proof, it captured the imagination of Europe, shifting public view from a card game toward fortune-telling and divination.

Etteilla (1789): The First Professional Tarot Reader

The Person: Jean-Baptiste Alliette, working under the pseudonym Etteilla, was a former seed merchant turned publisher, and the world's first professional, commercial Tarotist.

The Innovation: In 1789, amidst the chaos of the French Revolution, Etteilla published the first deck designed exclusively for divination rather than gameplay. He modified the old cards to align them closer to his vision of ancient Egyptian mysteries.

The Legacy: Etteilla invented the structural concepts modern readers take for granted: he assigned explicit keywords to every card, created the first formal layouts (spreads), and introduced reversed meanings (assigning a different interpretation if a card appears upside down).

Éliphas Lévi (1854): Synthesizing the Kabbalah

The Person: Alphonse Louis Constant, writing under the Hebrew name Éliphas Lévi, was a former Catholic seminarist turned radical occult philosopher.

The Innovation: In his 1854 book, Dogme et Rituel de la Haute Magie, Lévi made the ultimate structural breakthrough: he mapped the 22 Major Arcana cards directly onto the 22 letters of the Hebrew Alphabet. This linked Tarot irrevocably to the Kabbalistic Tree of Life and Western ceremonial magic.

The Legacy: Lévi popularized the iconic image of Baphomet, which later inspired the design of the Devil card. Without Lévi’s mathematical synthesis of the cards and Hebrew mysticism, the British Golden Dawn would have had no blueprint to build upon.

The Golden Dawn: Code-Breaking the Archetypes

Building on the mystical foundation laid in France, the Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn in late 19th-century London took de Gébelin's esoteric ideas and structured them into a cohesive system. They synthesized Tarot with Astrology, Alchemy, and the Kabbalah, mapping the 22 Major Arcana cards onto the paths of the cosmic Tree of Life.

By the late 1890s, the Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn revolutionized how cards were physically handled and interpreted. They shifted the focus away from simple fortune-telling toward high-level esoteric analysis. To do this, the Order established a comprehensive scriptural blueprint known as "Book T" (written by MacGregor and Moina Mathers), which paired specific shuffling operations with calculations based on astrology and the Kabbalah.

The Rider-Waite-Smith Milestone (1909)

Golden Dawn members Arthur Edward Waite and artist Pamela Colman Smith stripped away the remnants of the French playing card designs to create the Rider-Waite-Smith (RWS) deck. Smith replaced geometric pip patterns with deeply expressive, illustrated scenes, creating the visual standard for modern divination. Waite also published a companion book, The Pictorial Key to the Tarot, which provided the first widely accessible guide to Tarot symbolism. This deck and guidebook became the foundation for nearly all modern Tarot decks, cementing the Golden Dawn's influence on the practice of Tarot reading.

Carl Jung and the Archetypes of the Subconscious

In the 20th century, Swiss psychologist Carl Jung provided the analytical framework that validated what de Gébelin and the Golden Dawn sensed. Jung realized that human beings across all cultures share a Collective Unconscious filled with universal, inherited themes.

While Jung himself only mentioned Tarot passingly as containing "archetypes of transformation," modern analytical psychologists and Tarotists have fully synthesized his work. Today, the Major Arcana are recognized as brilliant, visible representations of Jungian Archetypes:

  • The Fool: The archetype of the innocent, new beginnings, and the spontaneous journey of the soul.
  • The Magician: The archetype of the trickster and the master of manifestation, representing skill and resourcefulness.
  • The High Priestess: The archetype of the wise woman, representing intuition, mystery, and the subconscious mind.
  • The Empress: The archetype of the primordial mother, nurturing, abundance, and fertility.
  • The Emperor: The archetype of the father, authority, structure, and stability.
  • The Hierophant: The archetype of the teacher, tradition, and spiritual guidance.
  • The Lovers: The archetype of choice, partnership, and the soulmate dynamic.
  • The Chariot: The archetype of the warrior, determination, willpower, and triumph.
  • Strength: The archetype of the inner hero, patience, courage, and resilience.
  • The Hermit: The archetype of the sage, introspection, and the search for truth.
  • The Wheel of Fortune: The archetype of fate, cycles, and the cosmic forces of change.
  • Justice: The archetype of the judge, absolute fairness, and karmic consequences.
  • The Hanged Man: The archetype of the martyr, voluntary surrender, and gaining a new perspective.
  • Death: The archetype of the transformer, final endings, and essential rebirth.
  • Temperance: The archetype of the alchemist, balance, moderation, and the integration of opposites.
  • The Devil: The archetype of the shadow, temptation, materialism, and unacknowledged bonds.
  • The Tower: The archetype of sudden catalyst, upheaval, and the breaking down of false structures.
  • The Star: The archetype of the visionary, hope, inspiration, and spiritual renewal.
  • The Moon: The archetype of the dreamer, illusion, anxiety, and the deep waters of the psyche.
  • The Sun: The archetype of the divine child, radiant joy, vitality, and conscious clarity.
  • Judgement: The archetype of self-reflection, the higher call, and spiritual awakening.
  • The World: The archetype of the completer, wholeness, fulfillment, and cosmic integration.

From Gaming Rules to Divinatory Spreads

When Tarot initially moved from a Renaissance parlor game into a tool for divination during the late 18th century, early practitioners did not use small, structured layouts like a "Three-Card Spread." Instead, early readers like Etteilla utilized sweeping, complex card operations. They would routinely shuffle and deal out nearly the entire 78-card deck across massive grids, tables, or concentric circles to form complete, synchronized life timelines for their clients.

The "Book T" System & The Opening of The Key

The Philosophy of "Book T"

According to the original Book T system, cards do not have flat, isolated meanings. Instead, the reading process behaves like an organic chemical reaction:

Elemental Dignities: Cards modify one another based on their elements (Fire, Water, Earth, Air). For example, a card from the suit of Swords (Air) sitting next to a card from the suit of Wands (Fire) becomes incredibly active and strong, whereas a card from the suit of Cups (Water) will visually "quench" and weaken a neighboring card from the suit of Wands (Fire).

The Spread: Before a single card is dealt, the reader manually extracts one Court Card from the deck to represent the person receiving the reading (the Significator), matching their physical description or astrological sun sign.

The Traditional Multi-Tiered Layout

The master layout detailed in Book T is known as The Opening of the Key. Rather than pulling cards into a small, fixed shape, it functions as a highly methodical, 5-operation esoteric layout system designed to systematically narrow down a question from the cosmic down to the personal:

  1. First Operation (The Four Piles):

    The deck is cut by the client into four distinct piles from right to left. These piles correspond directly to the letters of the Hebrew divine name, YHVH (Yod, Heh, Vav, Heh), representing the Four Worlds of the Kabbalah to show the spiritual domain of the question.

  2. Second Operation (The Twelve Houses):

    The cards are reassembled and dealt out into twelve distinct piles arranged in a massive circle. These piles correspond to the 12 Astrological Houses, mapping out the environmental influences surrounding the querent.

  3. Third Operation (The Twelve Zodiac Signs):

    The cards are gathered and distributed again into twelve piles, this time representing the 12 Signs of the Zodiac, starting from the sign associated with the client's Significator to look deeper into their personal traits and choices.

  4. Fourth Operation (The Thirty-Six Decans):

    The cards are dealt based on the 36 Decans of the zodiac, evaluating the precise astrological aspects, timing, and specific spiritual forces at play.

  5. Fifth Operation (The Tree of Life):

    The final layout deals the remaining cards onto the 10 Sephiroth positions of the Kabbalistic Tree of Life, synthesizing the final outcome of the inquiry.

  6. The Counting Method:

    Throughout these operations, the reader turns the targeted piles face up and physically counts through the cards using a specialized mathematical algorithm based on the card’s inherent numerical value (e.g., counting forward 11 steps for an Ace, 7 for a Princess, or 12 for a Knight) to map a continuous path of fate and uncover the final message.
