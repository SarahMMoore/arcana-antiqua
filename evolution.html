The Evolution of Tarot

Petrarch's Triumphs and the Major Arcana

Francesco Petrarch's 14th-century allegorical poem, I Trionfi ("The Triumphs"), and 15th-century northern Italian court records documenting early carte da trionfi card games.

The Renaissance Allegory

The 22 cards we know today as the Major Arcana were originally called "carte da trionfi" (cards of the triumphs). Their structure directly mirrors the psychological and spiritual hierarchy found in Francesco Petrarch's celebrated 14th-century poem, I Trionfi. In both the poem and the card game, each stage represents a force that conquers the previous one, charting a journey from earthly desire to divine eternity.

The Hierarchical Alignment

Petrarch's six poetic triumphs map directly to the historical imagery of the early Italian decks:

  • The Triumph of Love (Amor)

    Meaning: Represents youth, earthly desire, and instinct.

    Tarocchi Parallel:The Lovers card.

  • The Triumph of Chastity (Pudor)

    Meaning: Virtue, restraint, and mental control triumph over base desire.

    Tarocchi Parallel:The Chariot or Temperance cards.

  • The Triumph of Death (Mors)

    Meaning: The great equalizer that conquers all mortal achievements and social status.

    Tarocchi Parallel: The Death card (historically depicting popes and emperors trapped beneath the horse's hooves).

  • The Triumph of Fame (Fama)

    Meaning: Memory, legacy, and good deeds outlast physical mortality.

    Tarocchi Parallel: The Judgment or The Angel card, calling forth historical memory.

  • The Triumph of Time (Tempus)

    Meaning: Time eventually erodes all earthly fame, rendering human monuments to dust.

    Tarocchi Parallel: The Hermit card (originally called "Il Tempo" / Time, frequently carrying an hourglass).

  • The Triumph of Eternity (Eternitas)

    Meaning: The ultimate awakening, rising above the material world into permanent divine order.

    Tarocchi Parallel: The World card.

Medieval Archetypes and The Fool's Journey

Geoffrey Chaucer's 14th-century Middle English literary masterpiece, The Canterbury Tales, paired with 20th-century mythic storytelling frameworks regarding universal human paths.

The Framework Narrative

Before modern psychology defined archetypes, medieval literature used them to explore the human condition. A prime example is Geoffrey Chaucer's 14th-century masterpiece, The Canterbury Tales. Chaucer utilizes a classic framework narrative: a diverse group of pilgrims travel together on a spiritual journey, sharing stories that reveal their inner virtues, flaws, and motivations.

This mirrors the concept of The Fool's Journey in tarot. The Major Arcana acts as a visual pilgrimage. The Fool (Card 0) is the ultimate, naive traveler who walks through the world, encountering distinct archetypal figures to gain wisdom and achieve spiritual completion.

Historical Note: The Custom of "Tarocchi Appropriati" (1500s) Centuries before modern psychology or occultism viewed the deck as a system for charting human personality, Renaissance elites were already using the cards for interpersonal character mapping. In the mid-16th century, a popular parlor game called "tarocchi appropriati" emerged in Italian courts.

Courtiers would randomly draw a Trump card from the pile, and local court poets would immediately compose extemporaneous, witty verses matching the structural meaning of that specific card to the person's actual virtues, flaws, or social reputation.

Literary and Tarot Archetype Parallels

  • The Knight and The Chariot

    The Canterbury Pilgrim: The Knight represents the peak of medieval chivalry, discipline, honor, and military order. He is driven by duty and a strict moral code.

    The Tarot Connection: The Chariot (and The Emperor). This archetype signifies willpower, focused ambition, victory, control over opposing forces, and the physical mastery of the material world.

  • The Prioress and The High Priestess

    The Canterbury Pilgrim: The Prioress is an educated, refined nun. While she is quiet and bound to her religious structure, she carries an air of courtly romance and hidden depth.

    The Tarot Connection: The High Priestess. This archetype represents intuition, divine mystery, hidden knowledge, and a quiet retreat from the chaotic external world.

  • The Wife of Bath and The Empress

    The Canterbury Pilgrim: Wealthy, fiercely independent, and five-times-married, the Wife of Bath is unapologetic about her earthly desires, sexual power, and love of life.

    The Tarot Connection: The Empress. This archetype represents ultimate abundance, divine feminine power, maternal creation, and the sensory pleasures of nature and fertility.

  • The Parson and The Hierophant

    The Canterbury Pilgrim: Unlike the corrupt clergy members on the trip, the Parson is a genuinely holy, self-sacrificing country priest who leads his flock by moral example.

    The Tarot Connection: The Hierophant. This archetype represents traditional spiritual wisdom, formal education, orthodox religious guidance, and institutionalized morality.

  • The Pardoner and The Devil

    The Canterbury Pilgrim: The Pardoner is a manipulative church official who sells fake religious relics and official "pardons" for sins, driven entirely by greed and exploitation.

    The Tarot Connection: The Devil (or The Magician reversed). This archetype signifies spiritual deception, being chained to material greed, manipulation, and the toxic illusions that trap the human soul.

The Esoteric Shift and the French Occult Revival

Antoine Court de Gébelin's Le Monde Primitif (Volume 8, 1781), Jean-Baptiste Alliette's (Etteilla) early divination texts (1789), and Éliphas Lévi's Dogme et Rituel de la Haute Magie (1854).

The Enlightenment Transformation

The spiritual pilgrimage did not jump straight from the Middle Ages to the modern era. Its most critical evolution occurred in 18th-century France. During the Enlightenment, Tarot transformed from a literal medieval social ladder into a deeply symbolic map of the human subconscious. This shift was born in the aristocratic salons of Versailles and Paris, where rational science collided with a fascination for ancient mystery.

The Renaissance Salons: Madame de Pompadour's Versailles

To understand how Tarot became mystical, one must look to the French royal court of the mid-1700s.

  • The Culture: Madame de Pompadour, the official chief mistress and close advisor to King Louis XV, was a massive patron of the arts, literature, and the famous Parisian salon culture.
  • The Catalyst: Her salons hosted the intellectual elite alongside charismatic, mysterious figures like the alchemist Count of St. Germain. This unique environment blended Freemasonry, early archaeology, and high-society occultism, setting the stage for Tarot to be completely re-imagined.

Antoine Court de Gébelin (1781): The Egyptian Myth is Born

In 1781, a French scholar, former Protestant minister, and Freemason named Antoine Court de Gébelin forever changed the course of Tarot history.

  • The Discovery: While visiting a salon in Paris, de Gébelin observed a group of aristocrats playing the old game of Tarocchi. Looking at the ancient imagery, he concluded the cards were a surviving fragment of a lost ancient civilization.
  • The Theory: In the eighth volume of his massive encyclopedia, Le Monde Primitif, de Gébelin published the first documented essay claiming Tarot was the sacred, lost "Book of Thoth" from Ancient Egypt. He argued the Major Arcana were hieroglyphs containing timeless spiritual secrets.
  • The Impact: Though his theory lacked historical proof, it captured the imagination of Europe, shifting public view from a card game toward fortune-telling and divination.

Etteilla (1789): The First Professional Tarot Reader

  • The Person: Jean-Baptiste Alliette, working under the pseudonym Etteilla, was a French printmaker and the world's first professional, commercial Tarotist.
  • The Innovation: In 1789, amidst the chaos of the French Revolution, Etteilla published the first deck designed exclusively for divination rather than gameplay. He modified the old cards to align them closer to his vision of ancient Egyptian mysteries.
  • The Legacy: Etteilla invented the structural concepts modern readers take for granted: he assigned explicit keywords to every card, created the first formal layouts (spreads), and introduced reversed meanings (assigning a different interpretation if a card appears upside down).

Éliphas Lévi (1854): Synthesizing the Kabbalah

  • The Person: Alphonse Louis Constant, writing under the Hebrew name Éliphas Lévi, was a former Catholic seminarist turned radical occult philosopher.
  • The Innovation: In his 1854 book, Dogme et Rituel de la Haute Magie, Lévi made the ultimate structural breakthrough: he mapped the 22 Major Arcana cards directly onto the 22 letters of the Hebrew Alphabet. This linked Tarot irrevocably to the Kabbalistic Tree of Life and Western ceremonial magic.
  • The Legacy: Lévi popularized the iconic image of Baphomet, which later inspired the design of the Devil card. Without Lévi's mathematical synthesis of the cards and Hebrew mysticism, the British Golden Dawn would have had no blueprint to build upon.

The British Occult Movement and Jungian Psychology

The historical records of the Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn (London, 1888), the publication records of the Rider-Waite-Smith Tarot Deck (1909), and Carl Jung's Collected Works (Volume 9, Part 1) concerning Archetypes and the Collective Unconscious.

The Golden Dawn: Code-Breaking the Archetypes

Building on the mystical foundation laid in France, the Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn in late 19th-century London took de Gébelin's esoteric ideas and structured them into a cohesive system. They synthesized Tarot with Astrology, Alchemy, and the Kabbalah, mapping the 22 Major Arcana cards onto the paths of the cosmic Tree of Life.

The Rider-Waite-Smith Milestone (1909)

Golden Dawn members Arthur Edward Waite and artist Pamela Colman Smith stripped away the remnants of the French playing card designs to create the Rider-Waite-Smith (RWS) deck. Smith replaced geometric pip patterns with deeply expressive, illustrated scenes, creating the visual standard for modern divination.

Carl Jung and the Archetypes of the Subconscious

In the 20th century, Swiss psychologist Carl Jung provided the scientific framework that validated what de Gébelin and the Golden Dawn sensed. Jung realized that human beings across all cultures share a Collective Unconscious filled with universal, inherited themes. He explicitly recognized Tarot as an intuitive tool for exploring these psychological patterns, identifying the Major Arcana as visible representations of Archetypes:

  • The Empress / The Mother:

    The instinctual archetype of nurturing, nature, and raw creation.

  • The Emperor / The Father:

    Structure, authority, law, and societal order.

  • The Hermit / The Wise Old Man:

    The inner guide, introspection, and wisdom gained through isolation.

  • The Shadow (The Devil):

    The repressed, unacknowledged, or darker elements of our own personality.

The Secular Mirror

By combining 18th-century French esotericism, 19th-century British occultism, and 20th-century Jungian psychology, Tarot achieved its modern identity. Today, secular readers treat the cards as a psychological mirror. Much like Chaucer's medieval pilgrims sharing framework stories on the road, modern card layouts act as a personal storytelling catalyst—helping users map, unlock, and navigate their own subconscious "Fool's Journey."
